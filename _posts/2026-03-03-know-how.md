## Abstract

A longstanding objection to the possibility of artificial general intelligence is that “our background knowledge consists largely of skills for dealing with things and people”—that is, common sense—“rather than facts about them,” (Dreyfus 1992: xii; cf also Levesque 2017). Under this “commonsense-knowledge problem,” as philosopher Hubert Dreyfus defines it in the introduction to *What Computers Still Can’t Do*, it can be argued that no amount of training data can replace the embodied, world-lived experience that is at the heart of genuine human intelligence. AI can (perhaps) represent knowledge, i.e. knowing-that; humans recognize in each other both knowledge and knowing-how, that is the kind of metaknowledge that (56) “guides our changing sense of what counts as the relevant facts.” With that in mind, it seems to me that, when an English professor (Rotella 2025) describes components of an “AI-resistant” course in the ChatGPT era such as teaching process over product and nurturing a classroom community dynamic, the suggestions are—though without explicit reference to the AI critical tradition—an appeal to the ability of such courses to cultivate a humanities-informed know-how.

In this talk, I discuss two contemporary aspects of humanities method that speak to the cultivation of know-how in the university classroom: 1. drawing on the work of digital humanist Jentery Sayers, I discuss the idea of humanist-as-tinkerer (Sayers 2011); and 2. drawing on my own experience as a Latinist who develops computational language models, I discuss the idea of humanist-as-craftsman (cf. Mills 1980). Both tinkering and craftsmanship, I argue, demonstrate an honest curiosity about how knowledge structures and technologies work, reinforcing the know-how that characterizes humanities traditions worth preserving (cf. LaPensee and Kaya 2025) like critical thinking and interpretation as well as close reading and deep engagement.
